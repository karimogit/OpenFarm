## Summary

The long, hot season allows a spring crop and – near the coast – a second crop planted in midsummer
for autumn. Tomatoes grow best at about 21–27 °C by day and 16–21 °C at night, so the hottest weeks of
summer, humidity and disease pressure are the main problems rather than frost.

## Sowing

- Sow indoors **6–8 weeks before your last frost** in a light seed mix with plenty of light
  (spindly seedlings mean too little light). For a coastal autumn crop, sow again in late spring.
- Harden off for about a week before planting out.

## Planting out

- Coastal lowlands: **March–April**, plus an autumn planting in **early July**. Inland/upland areas:
  **May–June**, autumn crops not recommended.
- Use a site where tomatoes, peppers, eggplant or potatoes have not grown for **at least three years**
  to avoid soil-borne disease and nematodes.
- Space plants **60 cm** apart in rows **90 cm** apart; set them deep so only two or three sets of
  leaves show. Leggy transplants can be laid horizontally in a trench.

## Care

- Keep soil pH at 6.0–6.5 and side-dress with nitrogen three to four weeks after planting.
- Stake, cage or trellis (the "Florida weave") to keep fruit off the ground and air moving.
- Prune staked plants to one or two main stems; caged plants only once, to three or four stems.

## Varieties

Choose disease-resistant cultivars. Fusarium wilt is common: letters such as F, FF, FFF, V, N and
TSWV on the label show resistances. Most heirlooms have little resistance. Early determinate types
often stop producing before the long summer ends.

## Harvest

First fruit comes 52–90 days after transplanting, depending on cultivar. Pick fully coloured but firm
fruit. Do not refrigerate green fruit – ripen it at about 21 °C.
